Your coping plan

A short, structured plan you can fill in now — while things feel manageable — so it's ready if a harder moment comes. Based on a real, widely-used clinical framework, not something improvised for this site.

Adapted from the Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention (Stanley & Brown, 2012), the template used by the US 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and many hospital and community mental health services. View the original template.

1Warning signs

Thoughts, feelings, or situations that tell you a harder moment might be building.

2Things I can do on my own

Coping strategies that don't need anyone else — a walk, music, breathing, anything that's worked before.

3People and places that help, just by being around them

Not necessarily to talk about what's going on — just somewhere or someone that helps you feel less alone.

4People I can ask for help directly

People you'd actually tell if things got hard — name and how to reach them.

5Professionals and services

Your GP, a psychologist, or a crisis line — see the always-available list below for Australian services.

Making the environment safer

The original framework's final step is reducing access to anything you might use to harm yourself during a crisis — for example, asking someone you trust to hold onto medication or other means temporarily. This is genuinely one of the most effective parts of a safety plan, and it's worth doing with a person, not a form — a GP, counsellor, or crisis line (below) can talk through this with you properly.
